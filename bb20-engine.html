Part 1: Process · Verification

The layer that earns the trust

A research engine that only confirms is worse than no engine. These are findings the specialists called their strongest — killed before they could reach a brief. Every one was a claim a confident PLM could have carried into a room.

Four corpora were one

Consumer called Nike Icon sheerness a PATTERN across four retailers. Bazaarvoice syndicates brand reviews to 2,300+ retailers — likely one corpus counted three times. One cited page carries 3 ratings and 1 review. No base rate was ever set.

The tier wasn't empty

Marketplace claimed no premium rung above $60. The LeBron 8" lists at $90. And it inverts: that short runs −37% plus a stackable 25%. The tier isn't missing — it can't hold price.

A year-blind index

"Nike apparel fell 7%" matched no year. Apparel grew to $13.4B from $13.0B. The skeptic reproduced the cause: asked for FY26, the search index returns FY25 filings as FY26.

50 grams of force

The wet-cling paper is real and quoted correctly — but 48.94 cN is 0.489 N, on artificial skin, perception tested on the forearm. Comfort relevance verified. Performance relevance rejected.

One voice, not two

Product and Design "independently" found body-mapped mesh. Both read Nike marketing, five years apart. Counted as corroboration it would have inflated confidence. Count the capability once.

Participation, not fandom

Four agents built women's cases on merch growth — one 2024 figure counted four times. Girls' US high-school participation fell 432,833 → 395,708 through the boom. Shorts are a participation purchase.

Part 2: Output · Macro

Four assets. Never joined.

The macro finding is not a gap in the line. It is a gap between capabilities Nike already owns — each verified, each shipping, none of them connected inside a single basketball short.

Asset 01 · Verified

Aero-FIT

Mesh zones that "lift fabric off the skin, reducing cling even when the material is saturated" — via high-crimp yarn and stitch structure, not a coating. Shipping in basketball, running and football.

Asset 02 · Verified

A visible aperture language

In running, the lighter mesh zones are "intentionally visible to show where the extra breathability lives." Nike has already made the engineering the graphic — on a short, in another sport.

Asset 03 · Verified

Body-mapped women's mesh

WNBA venting placed by digital body maps of female sweat and cling zones, built because athletes were rolling waistbands to hack fit. April 2021. Never carried into consumer product.

Asset 04 · Verified

Stature-graded proportion

The 2026 Women's Basketball team catalogue publishes 6.25" inseam, 21.5" hip width, +1"/+2"/−0.75" stature grading. The team channel engineers proportion. The consumer channel sells inches.

The claim is aimed at the wrong metric

The literature says the failure athletes feel when soaked is adhesion — and that wicking, drying and air-permeability metrics do not explain it. Nike leads Aero-FIT with ">2x airflow," a transport metric, and publishes no number at all for the anti-cling claim that is the mechanically interesting one. The technology is right. The frame is wrong.

Growth is real; price-holding is not

Nike grew apparel units in FY26 with flat ASP, "offset primarily by higher discounts." Basketball grew double digits — in North America only. Meanwhile a permanent "Sale Under $25 Basketball Shorts" page runs on Nike.com, and markdown depth tracks door, not product. No new product solves that.

Part 3: Decision · Human gate

The machine stops here

The engine expands and sharpens the inputs around the brief. It does not make the call. Below is the honest division of labour on the three recommended opportunities.

What the machine knows

  • Aero-FIT exists, is verified clause by clause, ships in three sports, and claims cling reduction under saturation through yarn and stitch structure.
  • Nike's team channel publishes stature-graded specs. The consumer channel does not.
  • Athletes have rolled waistbands and tucked hems for a decade, from independent sources.
  • The $90 short discounts at the same rate as the $30 short.

What the machine believes

  • That the marketing frame is wrong rather than the technology — and that a published cling standard would be an ownable axis.
  • That consumers select inseam blind, and that this costs something.
  • That a decade-old hack is a designable unmet need rather than a preference.
  • That franchise discipline outranks new product. It cannot prove this.

What only you decide

  • Whether to open a measurement axis competitors can then test you against.
  • Whether a comfort claim — all the science supports — carries weight in a performance vocabulary.
  • Whether inseam is a fit variable or an identity declaration. The evidence genuinely splits, and the two readings demand opposite products.
  • Whether extending Aero-FIT off the Sabrina signature dilutes it.
