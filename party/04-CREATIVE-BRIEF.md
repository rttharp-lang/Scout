# PHASE 6 — THE CREATIVE BRIEF

# SAMPLE SIZE
### PROVENANCE, NOT PRODUCT.
`61 PAIRS · ONE ROOM · TWO HOURS`

---

## THE CONCEPT

Everyone brings one pair of their own shoes — not new, not product, the pair that means
something — and for two hours the room becomes a lit museum of the team's own taste,
curated by the team. Sixty-one black plinths under tight warm spots. On each, a pair and
a card the owner filled in themselves: name, city, year, one line about why. A sign
painter works a bone-white wall all night, adding a line to a running index as each pair
arrives, so the room's central document visibly completes as the party does. The bar
pours only single-estate, additive-free agave, labelled by rancho and vintage — the same
card, in a bottle. The food is one taco served three times, the corn changing each time,
each sleeve printed with the corn's name and the elevation it grew at — the same card, in
your hand. Nothing is branded, because everything is already labelled. And one plinth
stands lit and empty from the first minute with nobody explaining it, until minute 105.

---

## VISUAL MOODBOARD DIRECTION

Museum accession photography, not event photography. Objects shot straight-on against
seamless, evenly lit, ruler in frame, no styling. Institutional wall text set with
absolute discipline. Cold vitrine glass against warm brass. Herbarium sheets, mineral
specimen labels, the back of a Burgundy bottle, an equipment manager's inventory sheet, a
tahona wheel, a hand-painted market sign in Guadalajara, the underside of a 1994 sample
with a factory stamp on it.

Two image modes only, held rigidly apart: **objects, flat and clinical** — and **people,
35mm, direct flash, hard shadow, shot at chest height**. Never blend them. The contrast
between the machine-set grid and the one hand-lettered line is the identity.

---

## COLOR SYSTEM

| Role | Value | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Gallery bone | `#F2F0EA` | Walls, cards, print stock, the index wall |
| Ink | `#101010` | All type, all plinths, the bar face |
| Accession red | `#D6301F` | **Numbers only.** Plinth numbers, years, edition marks |
| Brass | `#B08D57` | Physical only — grommets, rail, bar hardware, the shoehorn |
| Vitrine grey | `#C9C6BE` | Hairlines, rules, secondary data |

The discipline is the rule, and it is the only rule: **red is reserved exclusively for
numerals.** Never a word, never a shape, never a fill. A viewer will not consciously
notice this and will feel it everywhere. This is the Ways-of-Working lesson translated —
confidence expressed as restriction.

Deliberately absent: the electric blue and lime the team already uses on Scout. That is
their day job. This is not.

---

## TYPOGRAPHY DIRECTION

- **Display** — Söhne Breit Kräftig. Extended, authoritative, set in caps at large scale
  with tight tracking. Used sparingly and always huge.
- **Data & labels** — Söhne Mono, 7–9pt, generous letter-spacing, aligned to a ruled
  baseline. This is the workhorse; 80% of type in the room is this.
- **The hand** — one sign painter's brush script on the index wall, and sixty guests'
  own handwriting in Blackwing pencil on the cards.

The whole typographic argument is machine-set grid versus one human hand. Never a third
voice. No script fonts, no faux-vintage, no condensed sports type.

---

## SIGNATURE GRAPHIC — THE PROVENANCE CARD

A 3×5in field, portrait, bone stock, letterpressed in ink. A 0.5pt rule 8mm from the top
carries the accession number, right-aligned, in accession red. Below it, four ruled
hairlines at 11mm intervals, each with a 7pt mono field label sitting flush left beneath
the rule: `OWNER` · `CITY` · `YEAR` · `WHY`. The `WHY` rule is the longest and runs the
full width. Nothing else. No logo, no border, no ornament.

It scales without modification from 3×5 in the hand to eight feet on the wall, and it is
applied to **every** object in the building: each pair of shoes, each bottle behind the
bar, each taco sleeve, the invitation, the drink menu, the food counter, the bathroom
door, and the takeaway. It is the entire brand system. There is nothing else to design.

---

## INVITATION CONCEPT

Mailed, not emailed — the single best-value decision on the whole event, and it must land
ten days out.

A glassine bag, 10×14in, sealed with a brass-toned foil dot. Inside: one provenance card,
letterpressed, with the `OWNER` field already filled in by hand — the guest's name — and
the other three rules left blank. On the back, in 8pt mono, four lines:

> `BRING ONE PAIR OF YOUR OWN SHOES.`
> `NOT NEW. THE ONES THAT MEAN SOMETHING.`
> `CARRY THEM IN THIS BAG.`
> `THURSDAY. 6:00. [ADDRESS].`

The bag is the instruction, the container, the arrival image and the takeaway vessel, all
at once. Sixty people walking in holding identical glassine bags is the first photograph
of the night and it costs nothing.

---

## ENTRANCE MOMENT

No check-in table. No name tags. No coat check line at the door. A short, tight, low-lit
approach — a corridor, not a lobby.

Two hosts stand at the threshold with a chilled marble slab of pre-poured coupes. A drink
is in a guest's hand within four seconds of the door, before anyone has spoken to them,
because someone decided for them and decided well.

Three steps in, a runner takes the glassine bag. Ten feet further, a rail at standing
height with a rack of Blackwing 602s: the guest fills in their own card — city, year, and
one line — standing up, in under ninety seconds. The runner walks the pair and the card to
the next numbered plinth while the guest is still writing. By the time the guest turns
around, their shoes are lit.

**Salt the room:** twelve people are inside with drinks in hand fifteen minutes before
doors. A room that is empty when you walk in makes you a spectator; a room at 20% makes
you a participant. This single move deletes the awkward first twenty minutes, and it is
free.

---

## SPACE PLAN

Roughly 3,500–5,000 sq ft, raw, hard floor, high ceiling, one long axis.

- **The corridor** (entry, 25ft) — dark, tight, tray and card rail.
- **The grid** (centre, the bulk of the room) — 61 plinths, 900mm high, 350mm square,
  matte black, in a loose 8×8 grid with 1.2m aisles. Each with a single tight warm spot.
  No seating and no bar anywhere near them: *standing with a drink next to an object* is
  the highest-yield conversational geometry that exists.
- **The Index wall** (long wall, 8ft × 12ft, bone) — ruled into 61 numbered rows. The sign
  painter works it all night.
- **The bar** (far end, deliberately the longest walk from the door) — the only thing
  everyone wants, at the opposite end of everything.
- **The plancha counter** (short wall) — two cooks, visible fire.
- **The neat table** (a discovered corner, unmarked, no bartender assigned).
- **The perimeter** — eight low pockets, two chairs and a lamp each, unlit until minute
  105. Seating for a third of the room, on purpose. Seat sixty and you get six frozen
  tables.

---

## BAR DESIGN

A twelve-foot surface, not a fortress — no raised front bar. Honed stone or a raw-edge
black walnut slab. Behind it, a single floating shelf at eye level holding fourteen
bottles, labels forward, spaced exactly four inches apart: the discipline of a gallery
hang, not the clutter of a liquor store. Fortaleza's wax tops and blown-glass agave
stoppers, Siete Leguas' plain paper label, Ocho's field-and-vintage label, G4's tall clear
bottle. They already look like objects. Add nothing.

A single warm 2700K LED strip *under* the shelf, raking down so each bottle throws a
shadow. The bar is the brightest thing in the room at eye level; everything else is
darker.

Behind the shelf, unlit, a 6×4ft matte black panel: the NOM numbers, ranchos and vintages
of every bottle poured, set in 12pt white mono, spaced like a stock ticker. From ten feet
it is a texture. From two feet it is a document. It is, of course, the same card.

---

## SIGNATURE TEQUILA DRINK — **HIGHLAND ROAD**

- 2 oz Siete Leguas Blanco (NOM 1120, Atotonilco el Alto — brick oven, 50/50 tahona and
  roller mill, no additives, ~$55)
- 0.75 oz lime, pressed within 4 hours
- 0.5 oz Ancho Reyes Verde — the poblano, not the red; herbal green heat, not sweet chile
- 0.5 oz agave nectar cut 2:1 with water
- 2 dashes 20% saline
- 1 oz Topo Chico, added at the pass

**Batch and hold:** everything but the Topo Chico batched, **pre-diluted 18% by volume**,
held at 28°F in an undercounter freezer. Service is one pour from a stainless pitcher over
a single cube, then a splash. No shaking, no jigger, no ice scoop. **Six seconds a drink.**
Two 5L batches = 60 drinks, made before doors.

Thin-lipped 10.5oz rocks glass, heavy base. One 2" hand-cut clear cube. A 4" lime peel
expressed and dropped, and one dehydrated poblano ring floated on the surface — dark
green, translucent, like a piece of jewellery.

**The detail that makes someone pick the glass up and turn it:** each cube has a single
dehydrated lime wheel frozen dead centre, cut from a slab poured with the wheels suspended
mid-depth. A green disc, suspended in perfectly clear ice, no bubbles.

**No rim salt anywhere.** A salted rim is the fastest way to read "tequila party." The
salt is dosed into the liquid. One small ceramic dish of *sal de gusano* sits on the neat
table with a bar spoon, for those who know what it's for.

---

## ZERO-PROOF — **AVENIDA**

Same rocks glass, same 2" cube with the lime wheel inside it, same poblano ring, same lime
peel. In the same rack. Indistinguishable at four feet.

Ritual Zero Proof agave alternative, lime, verjus blanc, roasted-pineapple-and-black-pepper
syrup, saline, Topo Chico. Same batch-and-hold, same six-second pour.

It appears on the menu in the same type size, in the same list, with no icon, no asterisk,
no "N/A." **This is non-negotiable — the moment you label it, you have made the person
holding it a category.**

---

## FOOD — **TRES MASAS**

One taco, three times. The corn changes at 0:15, 0:50 and 1:25 — three heirloom varieties
(bolita, cónico, olotillo), nixtamalized locally, pressed and griddled on-site at a
two-person counter. Four-inch tortilla, one hand, three bites, nothing that runs.

Handed directly off the plancha in a folded glassine-lined sleeve. No plates, no line
captain, no chafing dish anywhere in the building. Two cooks press 150–180/hr; sixty
people get three comfortable passes.

**Each sleeve is printed with only the corn's name and the elevation it grew at.** Nothing
else. The taco changes, the room notices, and people start asking what's coming next — the
storytelling doing free work. The card, in your hand, edible.

*Confirm the venue can take a 220V flattop or propane with a hood before falling in love
with this.*

---

## MUSIC

**Volume, not tempo, does the steering.** Most corporate parties run flat at 82–85dB — too
loud to talk, too quiet to dance. We start at 66dB and climb ~3–4dB per half hour.

| Window | BPM | dB | Territory |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–25 | 88–102 | 66–69 | Spiritual jazz, Brazilian — Azymuth, Alice Coltrane, Marcos Valle, Sault, Arthur Verocai. No English vocals for twelve minutes. |
| 25–55 | 100–112 | 70–74 | Boogie, modern soul — Leon Ware, Aaliyah, Kaytranada, Sade, Brandy. Bass enters. |
| 55–78 | 112–122 | 76–82 | Afrobeat, Afro-house, disco edits — Fela (edited to 6 min), Larry Levan edits, Chaka Khan extended, Frankie Knuckles. |
| 78–90 | 118–124 | 84–88 | **The percussion.** |
| 90–105 | 118–128 | 88–94 | Peak — Crystal Waters, Missy, Masters at Work, Robin S. One Dr. Dre at minute 92 and never return to that lane. |
| 105–119 | 108 → 96 | 88 → 74 | One last enormous record played to the end, then three descending: Sade, D'Angelo, Marvin Gaye. **Silence at 119.** |

**Do not play:** anything Bruno Mars, "September," "Mr. Brightside," "Crazy in Love,"
"Not Like Us," or any record that has appeared in a Nike spot. The room clocks it
instantly and the taste premise collapses in three minutes.

**Hire a real crate selector, not an event-company DJ** — $1,200–2,500 in Portland. Book
from a bar or a party you have actually been to. Contractually: **no microphone.** A dBA
meter taped to the target curve, visible at the booth. If you take one thing from this
document, take that.

## SURPRISE MOMENT — **THE PERCUSSION** (minute 78)

At the hinge from build into peak, a percussionist walks in from the crowd. Unannounced,
no introduction, no microphone, no stage. They stand on the floor among people and play
congas and timbales over the DJ for twelve minutes.

The room stops because the sound suddenly has a body in it, and because nobody was told.
It requires nothing from the audience — no participation, no clapping along, nobody is
called out. And it quotes the Afro-Latin percussion already running through the night, so
it lands as inevitable rather than as a bit. **$400–700.**

*Risk, stated plainly:* they must rehearse thirty minutes with the DJ or it is noise on
noise. Hire someone with residency experience playing over house sets, not a jazz drummer.
And tell them in advance: *if the room hasn't warmed by 78, we may not use you — you're
paid either way.*

---

## LIGHTING

Kill the house lights entirely. The design is contrast, not fill.

61 tight warm spots, one per plinth, narrow beam, sharp cut-off — the objects are the only
brightly lit things in the room and everything else falls away. Light haze so the beams
have body. One warm rake across the bar shelf. Two soft washes on the Index wall so the
sign painter can work. Nothing on the walls otherwise. No uplighting; uplighting the
perimeter is what people do instead of designing.

Two cues only, both unannounced: at **minute 78** the plinth spots dim 30% and the room
goes amber for the percussion. At **minute 105** the centre drops, and the eight perimeter
pockets come up. That second cue is the only instruction that works without words — it
redistributes sixty people into small groups without a syllable spoken.

*Confirm the haze policy in writing at booking. Never learn this at 3pm.*

---

## SIGNAGE

There is none. There are only labels.

Every piece of text in the room is a provenance card at some scale — set in 7–9pt mono on
bone, flush left, on a ruled hairline, mounted at 48 inches. Bar, bathroom, exit, all of
it. Nothing explains anything. Nothing tells anyone what to do.

**Any mechanic that requires a written instruction has already failed.** A sign telling
people how to enjoy themselves is the precise texture of the thing this party exists to
avoid.

---

## TABLETOP DETAILS

- Bare surfaces, waist-high, no linen anywhere.
- Bone cocktail napkins, letterpressed with a single ruled hairline and one 7pt field
  label: `WHY`. Nothing else. People will write on them, which is the point.
- No coasters. Cups go on wood and leave rings.
- Blackwing 602 pencils in brass cups at four points in the room — the card rail, both
  ends of the bar, and the Index wall.
- Staff in plain black work shirts, no branding, no polos, no lanyards.
- The host has no special treatment, no reserved anything, and no microphone. If they need
  to say something, they say it to eight people at a time, four times, with a drink in hand.

---

## PHOTO MOMENT

No photo booth. No backdrop. No prompt sign. No activation of any kind.

The photograph is **the grid shot down the row** — sixty pairs of shoes, lit and receding,
each on a black plinth with a red number. Nobody has to be told to take it. One
photographer works the room for three hours, briefed for texture and candids, forbidden
from grip-and-grins, shooting 35mm with direct flash. The images arrive as a bound set the
following week, not a shared drive that night.

---

## THE HERO MOMENT — **THE LAST PLINTH** (minute 105)

Plinth 61 stands lit and empty from the first minute, its card blank. Nobody explains it,
and nobody is allowed to.

At minute 105 the DJ lands the last enormous record and lets it finish. In the volume drop
that follows, the host walks to plinth 61, takes off the shoes they arrived in, sets them
down, writes their own card at the rail, and walks back into the party in socks. No
microphone. No gathering. The sign painter adds the sixty-first line to the Index wall and
the document is complete.

The room notices in waves, which is better than all at once.

*Lower-risk variant if the host would rather not:* the plinth holds a single shoe box,
unopened all night, containing a sample from the team's own archive that never shipped.
Same mechanic, same minute, one degree less personal — and meaningfully less memorable.
Recommend the socks.

---

## TAKEAWAY OBJECT

**Your card, and your shoes, in the bag they came in.**

At the exit, one person hands each guest their glassine bag with their pair inside and
their provenance card — their own handwriting, their own city, their own year, their own
one line — slipped into the front.

It satisfies every lever that makes an object worth keeping: the guest **made** it, it was
**witnessed**, it is **one of one**, and it had **a job** for two hours. By the time they
reach the door it isn't a favour, it's theirs, and taking it home isn't a decision.

*Optional, BEST tier only:* a solid brass CNC-turned shoehorn, six inches, edition number
stamped on the shank, in the bag. An apparel team giving out a beautifully made shoehorn is
funny, correct, and completely unbranded. $30–55/unit. It is not necessary. The card is
already the better object.

| **1:18** | **THE PERCUSSION** | Unannounced. Twelve minutes. Lights drop 30% and go amber. |

## WHERE THE MONEY ACTUALLY BUYS MEMORY

**Highest return, in order:** bar throughput (invisible when right, ruinous when wrong) ·
the mailed invitation ($1,400 that sets the expectation ten days early) · the first ninety
seconds — a tray at the door and no check-in table · the percussion at 78 · low end in the
sound system · plinth lighting.

**Money guests never notice:** perimeter uplighting, logo repetition past the third
placement, floral, printed menus, a photo backdrop, and — genuinely — expensive añejo in a
batched drink. Nobody clocks it. Put that money into the glass it is served in.

**Cheap things always noticed:** room temperature, the weight of the glass, ice clarity,
whether the music got louder at the right minute, the bathroom, and being handed something
on the way out.

## WHAT WILL ACTUALLY GO WRONG

**1 · People forget to bring a pair, and feel exposed.** The single real risk in the
concept. *Fix:* eight house plinths held blank, and a briefed runner who will card anything
a guest is wearing — a jacket, a watch, a ring. Nobody stands in that room empty-handed.
Non-negotiable.

**2 · Bar line at minute 20.** 60 guests pull ~190 drinks and 40% of them in the first
twenty-five minutes. *Fix:* 90 coupes pre-poured, both cocktails batched and pre-diluted at
six seconds a pour, three bartenders and a barback, one roaming tray.

**3 · Intake becomes a queue.** Two runners and one card rail is not enough at a 6:00
arrival spike. *Fix:* three runners, two rails at opposite ends of the corridor, and cards
pre-filled with the owner's name so only three fields remain.

**4 · The percussion dies in the noise.** *Fix:* a hard music cue, a lighting change, two
staff physically opening a lane, and thirty minutes of rehearsal before doors. If the room
hasn't warmed by 78, cut it — they are paid either way.

**5 · Headcount is not 60.** Plus-ones push it to 75; a deadline drops it to 44. *Fix:*
contract food and bar at 60 with a 72-hour call-up, and order cards, bags and plinth
numbers at 80 from day one. The overage costs less than the shortfall.

**6 · Someone's shoes go home with the wrong person.** Genuinely the worst outcome
available, because it is the one thing a guest cannot be compensated for. *Fix:* the plinth
number is written on the glassine bag at intake and on the card. Nothing leaves without a
matching number.
