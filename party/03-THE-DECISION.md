# PHASE 5 — CREATIVE DIRECTOR DECISION

## WE ARE MAKING **SAMPLE SIZE**.

**It beats PRESS ROW because Press Row is a party about being looked at, and Sample
Size is a party about looking.** Press Row's hero moment — the live darkroom — is a
single point of failure with a chemistry problem, arriving at minute 100 with no
substitute if it fails. More importantly, a room of people being photographed all
night produces a great set of images and a thin evening; the shy third of the team
spends two hours managing their face. Sample Size photographs beautifully *as a
by-product* of something else being true.

**It beats THE MORGUE FILE because The Morgue File is the better idea and the worse
decision.** It is the most original thing eight agents produced and I would fight for
it in a different year. But it is one bad week from being a public post-mortem: if a
cancellation or a reorg is fresh, a wake for dead product is the most tone-deaf party
of the decade, and the host cannot control that timing four weeks out. It also hands
any stakeholder in the approval chain an obvious, defensible reason to kill it. A
concept whose risk sits outside the producer's control is not a concept, it's a bet.

**And it wins on its own terms, not just by elimination.** Three things only Sample
Size does:

**One — it makes the guests the content.** Sixty people whose profession is taste each
bring their single best object. No decor budget competes with that, and no agency can
fake it. It is structurally impossible for HR to have produced this room, which is the
exact thing the host asked for.

**Two — it scrambles status without anyone noticing.** The junior designer with a beat
pair from a specific afternoon in 2011 becomes the most interesting person in the room
for ten minutes. That is the whole assignment — *how do sixty coworkers talk to each
other differently* — solved by a mechanism instead of an icebreaker.

**Three — it has one idea, and the idea makes everything else.** Provenance. A
single-estate tequila labelled by rancho and vintage, a pair of shoes labelled by city
and year, and a taco sleeve printed with the corn's name and its elevation are the
same card. The bar, the food, the walls, the takeaway and the guests all speak one
sentence. Nothing needs to be branded, because everything is already labelled.

No hedge. This one.

# THE FINAL TEST

**Could this party belong to another company?**
No. It requires a room of people whose relationship to footwear is simultaneously
professional and personal, and who own objects with stories. A bank cannot run this.
Neither can a tech company. Neither, notably, can most of Nike — this is specifically
a *Basketball Apparel* party.

**Could this be mistaken for a normal corporate event?**
No. There is no microphone, no name badge, no check-in table, no step-and-repeat, no
agenda, no swag table, and no moment where anyone is asked to participate in anything.
The single most memorable act of the night is a leader taking their own shoes off.

**Is there one detail people will describe the next morning?**
Two, and they can each be said in one sentence. *"Everyone brought a pair of their own
shoes and they lit them like a museum."* And: *"At the end she took her own shoes off,
put them on the last plinth, and spent the rest of the party in socks."*

**Does every touchpoint feel like it came from the same creative world?**
Yes — because they are literally the same object. The provenance card is the plinth
label, the drink menu, the taco sleeve, the invitation, the wall, and the takeaway. One
ruled format at every scale from 3×5 to eight feet.
